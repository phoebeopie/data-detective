🤔 Why do the two tests disagree?

Months aren't independent: a dry month is usually followed by another dry month. The straight-line test treats every month as fresh evidence, so it can be over-confident. Mann-Kendall here is corrected for this, so it's more cautious — trust it more.

⏳ Does a longer record change the verdict?

Move the start year. Short records often can't prove a trend — even a real one.

How the detective works

Four ideas you need to read the evidence.

1. What is a trend?

A steady move in one direction over many years — rising, falling, thickening, thinning. Not the ups and downs of a single year.

The same thing can rise in one place and fall in another, even when one process causes both. That's why where you look matters.

2. Why remove the seasons?

Summers are hot and winters are cold every year. That big wiggle can hide a small, slow change. We subtract each month's long-term average, so January is compared with other Januaries.

3. What does "significant" mean?

The p-value is the chance of seeing a trend this strong if nothing were really changing. Below 0.05 (5%), we call it significant.

Not significant doesn't mean "no change" — it means the evidence isn't strong enough yet. A longer record often settles it.

4. The Mann-Kendall test

Compare every month with every later month and count how often things went up versus down. If "down" wins by far more than chance would explain, the trend is significant.

Sen's slope gives the speed: the middle value of all the month-to-month slopes. Both ignore extreme outliers.

Data: NASA Giovanni (GLDAS, MERRA-2, GPM, OMI, AIRS, MODIS) and NOAA Climate Data Online. Method: seasonal anomalies, least-squares trend with 95% confidence interval and t-test, Mann-Kendall test with Hamed-Rao autocorrelation correction (12-month lag), and Sen's slope.
